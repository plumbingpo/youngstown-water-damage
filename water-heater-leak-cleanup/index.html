First Five Minutes

Before We Arrive

  1. Cut the Power or GasIf the heater is electric, turn its breaker off. With a gas heater, the knob on the control goes to the off position. Running the burner or the element with no water in the tank is something to avoid every time.
  2. Shut the Cold Water ValveFind the cold water shutoff above the heater and turn it clockwise. If it is stuck or leaking, shut off the main water valve for the house.
  3. Beware of Scalding WaterTank water is hot enough to scald. Let pooled water cool off before anyone steps in it, and keep kids and pets out of the area.
  4. Empty the Tank OutsideWhen the drain valve is within reach, connect a garden hose and lead it to a floor drain or outdoors, which keeps the rest of the water off the floor.
  5. Move Stored ItemsLift boxes, rugs and anything absorbent off the wet floor. Fabric and cardboard absorb water fast, then keep it pressed to the concrete and walls.
The Usual Culprits

How It Usually Starts

  • Drain valves that crack, work loose or stop sealing completely.
  • Flex connectors or inlet and outlet fittings at the top of the heater that have corroded.
  • Pressure rising too high in the tank because the thermostat failed or no expansion tank was installed.
  • Anode rods that are used up, leaving the steel inside the tank open to rust.
  • An overflow pan that cannot drain, because its line is clogged or the pan itself has rusted.
What Is at Risk

What the Water Damages

Closet Walls

When the heater closet is indoors, its drywall draws water upward and passes it to rooms on the far side. Baseboards swell and the lower wall stays wet inside.

Flooring in Nearby Rooms

Water leaving a heater closet runs under the door and into carpet, laminate or hardwood in the hall. Heat makes it worse, with quicker swelling of wood boards and carpet backing that comes apart.

Stored Belongings

Whatever is kept by a closet or garage heater, from keepsakes to furniture and boxes, is the first thing to get wet. Wet contents may grow mold unless they are dried or thrown out soon, and cardboard simply collapses.

Ceilings Under an Attic Heater

A heater in the attic sends water through insulation onto the ceiling below. Wet insulation stops insulating and its weight can bring ceiling drywall down.

What We Do

Our Water Heater Leak Cleanup Process

1

Extract the Water

Our crew extracts the water standing in the closet, the garage or on the floor, plus the overflow pan and the cavity above the ceiling on attic jobs, so that materials around it quit absorbing it.

2

Inspect the Surrounding Area

Using moisture meters and thermal imaging, we check the platform, wall bases, adjoining rooms and any ceiling below to find the full extent of the wet area.

3

Take Out What Will Not Dry

Soaked insulation, crumbling drywall and damaged baseboards are taken out. Attic jobs include removing wet insulation to expose the joists and the ceiling drywall for drying.

4

Dry and Monitor

We keep the drying equipment going for the usual 3 to 5 days needed to dry the space. Moisture readings are recorded, and you hear from us once the space can take the new heater.

Coverage

Insurance and Water Heater Leak Cleanup

Water damage to belongings, walls and floors from a tank that fails suddenly is often covered as a sudden and accidental loss. Usually the unit is left out of the claim, as age related wear is viewed as a maintenance matter. Because coverage usually does not extend to gradual leaks, a claim may be denied or reduced when the adjuster sees indications the tank leaked for a long time, like old stains or trails of rust. Until the claim is closed, hold on to the old heater. Photograph it along with the damage, and review your policy.
