Dealing With Appliance Leak Water Damage in Youngstown

Three appliances cause most of the leaks we see. When the supply hose on a washing machine splits, water flows under full pressure until somebody turns it off. With a dishwasher the leak is typically slow, coming from the door seal or the drain hose where the cabinets hide it. Behind a refrigerator, the thin tube feeding the ice maker may drip for months. Cleanup is different for each of them.

Our Youngstown crew starts by pulling the appliance out and finding where the water went. For a kitchen, the usual places are below the flooring and under the cabinets. Laundry room water goes into the wall at the back of the machine and the neighboring rooms, and with an upstairs laundry, the ceiling underneath. We extract what is standing, measure what has soaked in, and set drying equipment sized to the wet area.

First Five Minutes

What to Do Right Now

  1. Turn Off the ApplianceIf the outlet and the floor next to it are both dry, end the cycle and take the plug out of the wall. Otherwise, use the breaker to cut its power.
  2. Shut Off the Water LineWashers have hot and cold valves on the wall behind them. Dishwashers shut off under the sink. A refrigerator line has a small valve below or behind the unit.
  3. Mop Up What You CanUse towels or a mop on whatever water is within reach, which keeps it from spreading beneath flooring and cabinets. Use a vacuum only if it is a wet and dry model.
  4. Keep the Door ClosedA dishwasher or washer that still holds water should stay closed. When the door opens mid-cycle, the full load of water can pour out on the floor.
  5. Document What FailedTake pictures of the hose, valve or fitting that failed and keep the part. Your insurer and the appliance maker may both ask to see it.
Why It Happens

Why This Happens

  • The water inlet valve on a washer or dishwasher failing to close, which lets the machine overfill.
  • The drain hose of the washer pulling free of the standpipe, or the standpipe backing up from a clog.
  • Overloading the drum or adding excess detergent, either of which can make the machine overflow.
  • Installation mistakes like a loose supply line connection or a dishwasher that does not sit level.
  • Plastic or copper ice maker lines that kink, crack or loosen at the fitting.
What Is at Risk

What the Water Damages

Wall Bases and Trim

Water standing on a laundry or kitchen floor wicks up into baseboards and the bottom of the drywall. The wall behind the appliance is often the wettest because nobody could see it.

Cabinet Damage

Dishwasher leaks soak cabinet sides and toe kicks from underneath. Swelling and separation follow, and for weeks the soaked cabinet boxes may trap moisture against the wall.

Hidden Mold at the Back

Hardly anyone looks at the dark, warm space behind a dishwasher or refrigerator. A slow drip there feeds mold on the wall and floor long before anyone notices.

Downstairs Ceiling Damage

The ceiling under an upper floor laundry catches whatever water makes it past the floor above. Drywall there sags and stains while the insulation over it holds water.

Will Insurance Pay?

Does Insurance Cover It?

When a machine overflows unexpectedly or a hose bursts, that is typically treated as sudden and accidental, which many homeowners policies cover. What is typically paid is the water damage, while the failed part and the appliance are left out. A claim is harder when an ice maker line or dishwasher seeped slowly, since coverage usually does not extend to gradual leaks. How old the hose was and how it was maintained may also come up with some insurers. Before cleanup, take photos, save the failed part and read your policy.

What It Costs

What Affects the Cost

  • Duration of the leak, particularly a hidden drip that went unnoticed behind the machine.
  • How many rooms the water reached, including any ceiling below an upstairs laundry.
  • The appliance involved, and if the leak was a slow seep or a fast flood.
  • If the flooring and cabinets have to come out or can be dried where they are.
